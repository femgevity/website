The Reimbursement Reality

The codes exist. The pathway doesn't.

Medicine can code the symptom. Medicine can code the disease. What no code describes is the decade in between — when the trajectory is still modifiable and nobody is paid to change it.

What gets coded

The symptom state

Menopausal and climacteric states are codeable as a condition. The code captures that she is in transition. It does not capture what that transition is doing to her cardiovascular, skeletal, metabolic, or neurocognitive risk.

N95.1N95.8N95.9
What doesn't
The decade in between

Longitudinal management of the disease burden driven by untreated hormonal transition. Existing codes describe a symptom on one end and a diagnosed disease on the other. Nothing in the middle describes the work of keeping her from crossing that line — the repeat panels, the risk stratification, the titration, the tracking against her own baseline.

Reimbursement for the full workup is applied inconsistently across payers. The economics of doing it properly push clinicians out of insurance models entirely — not out of greed, but because stepping outside is the only way to buy the time the work actually takes.

What gets coded again

The endpoint

Cardiovascular disease. Osteoporosis. Type 2 diabetes. Cognitive decline. Every one is fully codeable, fully reimbursable, and fully expensive — by the time it qualifies, the preventable window has closed.

I25M81E11G30

The result is that the decade in between stops being unbillable work your clinicians absorb at a loss, and starts being a documented, reportable, reimbursed service line inside your practice. Michele Wispelwey, MBA — Founder & Chief Executive Officer

She had anxiety. Depression. Weight she could not explain. Her cardiovascular markers were moving. Her metabolic markers were moving.

Every one of those signals was raised. Every one was explained away — fibroids, or stress, or the change.

It was cancer. By the time anyone looked properly it had reached her liver. She was gone in three months.

Michele Wispelwey has since lost her father, her grandparents, her aunts, and her grandmother. The pattern repeated each time: signals across several systems at once, each one small enough to dismiss on its own, each one belonging to a different specialist, and no one assembling them into a picture.

Not one of those physicians was careless. Not one of them had been given the tools, the time, or the reimbursement to look further.

She spent the next eighteen years inside the diagnostics industry — Quest Diagnostics and LabCorp, a $40M client portfolio, a women's health platform she led to a $60M acquisition. She carried molecular genetics, cancer biomarkers, and advanced PCR into New York's leading physician networks, and helped launch the metro area's first saliva-based COVID-19 PCR test.

So she knew exactly what advanced diagnostics could do. She had spent two decades putting them into physicians' hands. And she knew exactly which women were never in front of them.

The science existed. The panels existed. What did not exist was a way to put them inside the practices women already trusted, covered by the insurance they already carried — so that looking further stopped depending on whether a woman could afford to go looking on her own.
